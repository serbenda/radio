If you’ve caught the bug

How to get started in Spain

  1. Listen (it’s free and needs no licence)

    Use the web receivers on the Listen page. If you’d like your own, a USB SDR receiver costing around €30-40 and some free software turn your computer into a radio that can hear almost anything.

  2. Find a radio club

    It’s the quickest way to learn. The URE (Unión de Radioaficionados Españoles, the Spanish national society; website in Spanish) has sections all over Spain, and many clubs run courses for the exam.

  3. Pass the exam

    There are questions on basic electricity and radio, regulations and operating practice. When you pass you get the HAREC certificate, which is valid in much of Europe.

  4. Apply for your authorisation and call sign

    With the certificate, you apply to the Ministry for your amateur radio authorisation, which assigns you your call sign (something like EA4XYZ). That’s your “name” on the air.

  5. Set up your station and make your first contact

    To have your own equipment at home, you apply for a station licence with a description of your antennas and equipment. And then: “CQ, CQ…”.

Your checklist

Listening only · ~€35

USB SDR receiver

A “dongle” that plugs into your computer. With a simple antenna you can hear aircraft, ships, weather satellites and radio amateurs. No paperwork at all.

Radio with no exam

What about CB-27 and walkie-talkies?

Before amateur radio, or instead of it, there are two ways to talk on the radio with no exam or licence: Citizens Band (CB-27) and PMR446 walkie-talkies. Lots of radio amateurs started out this way.

Common use · no licence

CB-27, Citizens Band

A band set aside so that anyone can talk on the radio for fun, around 27 MHz. In Spain it has been common use (uso común) since 2015: you don’t need an exam, a licence or a call sign. An approved radio is all it takes.

  • 40 channels of 10 kHz, from 26.960 to 27.410 MHz.
  • Channel 9 (27.065 MHz): distress and emergencies. If an emergency is in progress, it must be kept clear.
  • Maximum power: 4 W on FM and AM, 12 W peak on single sideband (SSB).
  • Antenna: up to 6 dB gain, radiating equally in all directions.
  • Direct communication only between radios (no repeaters), and not for profit.

Amplifiers or anything exceeding that power are not allowed, nor are repeaters, satellite links, connecting it to the telephone network or transmitting a continuous carrier.

Common use · no licence

PMR446 walkie-talkies

The walkie-talkies sold in shops and supermarkets. They work on 446 MHz, with 0.5 W and a fixed antenna, also with no licence. Their range is short: from a few hundred metres in town to a few kilometres in open country.

Before you buy anything

Which radio to buy (and what’s legal)

The golden rule: listening is free; to transmit you need the right equipment and, outside CB and PMR446, your licence. A cheap walkie-talkie doesn’t give you permission to talk on any frequency you like.

I want to…Licence?With what
Listen to radio amateurs, CB, broadcast stationsNoA web receiver, a USB SDR receiver or any receiver
Talk to my family or on a day outNoApproved PMR446 walkie-talkies (0.5 W, fixed antenna)
Talk on Citizens BandNoAn approved CB-27 radio (max. 4 W, 12 W on SSB)
Transmit on 2 m and 70 cm, repeaters, satellitesYesAn amateur radio (Baofeng, Quansheng, Yaesu…) and your call sign
Talk on police, marine, aviation or business frequenciesNeverNot allowed with any amateur equipment

Any equipment that transmits must have its conformity assessment (CE marking). And even if a receiver picks up everything, communications not intended for the public (emergency services, private conversations) are protected by the secrecy of communications (Article 18.3 of the Spanish Constitution): you don’t listen to them on purpose, and you never pass on or use what you hear.

Baofengs: what you should know

The Baofeng UV-5R and its siblings are the best-selling amateur handhelds in the world thanks to their price (around €20-30). They cover the 2 m and 70 cm bands and quite a lot more. That has a good side and a bad side:

✅ Legal

Listening with it. And, with your amateur licence, transmitting on the amateur bands (144-146 MHz and 430-440 MHz).

❌ Not legal

Using it without a licence “like a walkie-talkie”: it isn’t a PMR446 (more power and a removable antenna). Nor transmitting outside the amateur bands, even if it technically can.

⚠️ Watch the quality

Reviews agree that its receiver gets overloaded near powerful antennas and that its transmitter produces spurious emissions. Germany banned its sale in 2021.

👍 What it’s good for

As a cheap first radio for learning to program channels, listening to repeaters and making your first contacts once you’re licensed.

Our recommendation

  1. Not licensed yet: listen for free on the web receivers and, if you get hooked, buy a USB SDR receiver. To talk to your family and friends, get some PMR446 radios. Don’t buy a Baofeng to talk without a licence.
  2. Freshly licensed: an inexpensive dual-band to start with; if you can stretch a little, the TD-H3 receives quite a bit better than the UV-5R.
  3. In it for the long haul: a brand-name radio (Yaesu, Icom, Kenwood) and, later on, a shortwave radio, new or second-hand.
  4. Before buying, ask at your radio club: there’s nearly always someone who’ll let you try their radio and help you program it.

    For parents

    Can a child get a licence?

    Yes, and the regulations set no minimum age. For their station licence, the child provides a written authorisation from their parents or guardians, who take on the responsibility (Article 19).

    Is it safe?

    Everything said on the radio is public: anyone can listen. That’s why you give your town, never your address, and don’t talk about personal matters.

    What about antennas and electricity?

    Putting up outdoor antennas or handling power supplies should be done with an adult or at the radio club. It’s part of the exam.

    Are there activities for young people?

    Yes: the YOTA programme runs camps and the December YOTA Month for under-26s, and there’s the Jamboree on the Air with the Scouts.

    For teachers and group leaders

    Radio in the classroom

    A 50-minute session for young people aged 10 to 16, using this site and a printable worksheet. No radio knowledge or equipment needed: a projector or a few phones is enough.

    🕐 Session plan

    1. 5 min · Hook: move the dial on the home page and play a station from another continent.
    2. 10 min · What is that sound?: play sounds without saying which; in groups, they guess by looking at the waterfall. Then a real recording.
    3. 10 min · Morse: everyone writes their name in Morse on the worksheet and “sends” it to a partner by clapping.
    4. 10 min · Spell it like on the radio: in pairs, they spell a word with the phonetic alphabet; the other writes it down.
    5. 10 min · First contact: in pairs, they fill in the script on the worksheet and read it aloud; then the simulated contact on the site.
    6. 5 min · Wrap-up: what is radio for when everything else fails? Link to “Emergencies”.

    To go further: invite a nearby radio club (see “Find your radio club”). Many give demonstrations in schools, and some help schools contact the ISS.

    When everything goes down

    The radio that doesn’t depend on anything

    With no mobile signal, no power and no internet, a radio with a battery and a length of wire keeps working. In Spain, volunteers from REMER (Red Radio de Emergencia, Civil Protection’s emergency radio network) and from radio clubs support communications during disasters. Many people come to amateur radio this way: wanting to be useful.
