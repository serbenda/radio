Beyond amateur radio

Spectrum

The airwaves are divided up like a street map: every frequency has an owner. Here you'll see who talks on each one (CB, walkie-talkies, emergency networks, aircraft, ships, satellites), how they sound, what to listen with, what is legal to listen to and, at the end, everything about antennas.

For anyone

Licence-free radio

Some bands are for common use: anyone may transmit on them, with no exam or paperwork, as long as they use type-approved equipment and stick to the power limit. They are many people's way into radio.

27 MHz · common use

CB, Citizens Band

It was born in the United States after the Second World War, became a craze there in the 1970s and in Spain in the 1980s, when lorry drivers filled it with nicknames and road talk. It's still alive among lorry drivers, country folk, DX fans (long-distance contacts) and anyone who wants to talk on the radio without an exam.

How it works

  • 40 channels of 10 kHz between 26.960 and 27.410 MHz.
  • Three modes: AM (the classic), FM (cleaner over short distances) and SSB, single sideband (goes further with the same power; the favourite for long-distance contacts).
  • Power: 4 W in AM and FM; 12 W peak in SSB.
  • Channel 9 (27.065 MHz): distress. It is always kept clear when there's an emergency.
  • Direct only: radio to radio, with no repeaters and no links to the internet or the phone network.

How far it goes

With a radio in the car, 5 to 20 km depending on the terrain; from a house with a good antenna, more. As it sits right next to the 10-metre amateur band, it behaves the same way: in the good years of the solar cycle the ionosphere bounces it back to earth and you hear stations from other countries, and in summer there are sudden openings thanks to sporadic E.

The equipment

A type-approved mobile or base radio (CE marking, European standard EN 300 433) and, above all, a good antenna: the wavelength is 11 metres, so a quarter-wave vertical is about 2.7 m long. Car antennas are usually shorter, with a coil that "lengthens" them electrically at some cost in performance. More in antennas.

Not allowed on CB in Spain: amplifiers, exceeding the power limit, repeaters, connecting it to the phone network and transmitting a continuous carrier. Since 2015 no licence or call sign is needed (Orden IET/614/2015, note UN-3 of the national frequency allocation table).

446 MHz · common use

PMR446 walkie-talkies

The walkie-talkies from toy shops, hiking stores and building sites. 16 analogue FM channels between 446.0 and 446.2 MHz, 0.5 W and a fixed antenna (it can't be changed). There are digital versions (dPMR446 and DMR446) in the same band, which can't talk to the analogue ones.

Range: from a few hundred metres among buildings to several kilometres with a clear line of sight; from a summit, much further.

"Privacy codes" are not private. They are sub-tones (CTCSS or DCS): your walkie only opens its speaker when the chosen tone arrives, but anyone on the same channel without a tone hears you perfectly. They stop you hearing others, not others hearing you.

433 MHz and more · short range

Short-range devices

Garage remotes, wireless doorbells, home weather stations, sensors and 10 mW LPD walkie-talkies use bands such as 433 MHz (433.05–434.79 MHz) or 868 MHz, at very low power and with no licence.

Wifi and Bluetooth are licence-free radio too, at 2.4 and 5 GHz. With a cheap SDR receiver you can see all of this on the waterfall: short bursts every time someone opens a garage or a sensor sends the temperature.

With a business licence

Professional and emergency networks

Taxis, ambulances, police, fire brigades, metros, ports or a big building site use radio because it's instant: press and your whole group hears you at once, with no dialling or waiting, and it works even if the mobile network goes down. Each organisation has frequencies assigned by the authorities. These networks are not intended for the public (see what you may listen to).

Analogue

Analogue PMR

Good old radio: FM on 12.5 kHz channels in VHF (around 146–174 MHz) and UHF (400–470 MHz), with repeaters on high ground. Cheap and simple; used by private security, building sites, councils and transport. More and more of it is going digital.

Digital · 12.5 kHz

DMR

The European (ETSI) standard most used by businesses: each 12.5 kHz channel carries two conversations at once, taking turns in 30-millisecond slots. It has three tiers: DMR446 (licence-free), conventional and trunked (a network that shares out the channels). Radio amateurs use it too, on their own bands.

Digital · 6.25 kHz

dPMR and NXDN

DMR's siblings which, instead of taking turns, use narrower 6.25 kHz channels. Popular with small businesses, hospitality and events.

Digital trunked · mission-critical

TETRA, the radio of the emergency services

TETRA (Terrestrial Trunked Radio) is a European standard from 1995 designed for the emergency services. It doesn't work like a walkie-talkie but like a mobile phone network for walkie-talkies: many base stations, a central system that shares out the channels, and terminals that move from one cell to another without dropping.

What makes it special

  • Instant group calls: press and everyone in your group (a police station, a fire crew) hears you in under a second.
  • Four conversations per channel: each 25 kHz channel is split into four time slots (TDMA), with digital phase modulation (π/4-DQPSK) at 36 kbit/s gross.
  • Emergency button that overrides any other call.
  • Direct mode: with no network (a forest fire, a tunnel), terminals talk to each other like walkie-talkies.
  • Encryption over the air and end to end: police conversations can't be heard.
  • Slow data: messages, GPS position and status, but no video (about 28.8 kbit/s at most).

Who uses it in Spain

  • Regional emergency networks, in the 380–400 MHz band that Europe reserves for public safety. For example RESCAT in Catalonia: over 60,000 users (regional police, local police, firefighters, paramedics, civil protection) and 321 base stations.
  • Local police, fire brigades and civil protection in many towns.
  • Transport and large sites: metros, airports, ports, on other 400 MHz bands.
  • The national network, SIRDEE (National Police, Guardia Civil and others, over 150,000 users), uses TETRAPOL, a sibling system of French origin that shares channels out by frequency rather than by time slot.

What it looks like

On an SDR receiver's waterfall, a TETRA base station is a 25 kHz-wide block that never switches off: it transmits its control channel non-stop so terminals know there's a network. Tuned in FM, it sounds like a continuous raspy buzz. You can hear an imitation in how they sound.

The future: emergency networks are starting to move to mission-critical broadband (push-to-talk, video and data over 4G and 5G with priority). TETRA will stay for years as the backup voice network, because it keeps working when everything else fails.

Volunteering

When everything fails: amateur radio in emergencies

If power, mobile and internet go down, a battery-powered radio and a wire antenna keep working. That's why Spain's civil protection has REMER (the emergency radio network), made up of volunteer radio amateurs, and many clubs run drills. It's one of the best reasons to get licensed: get started.

What flies and what sails

Aircraft and ships

Aviation and shipping have used voice radio for a century, and today they also broadcast their position automatically, so other aircraft, other ships and anyone with a receiver can see them.

118–137 MHz · AM

✈️ Airband

Pilots and controllers talk in AM, not FM. That's deliberate: if two transmit at once, in AM you hear both on top of each other (and someone notices); in FM the stronger one would blot out the other without anyone knowing. In Europe channels are 8.33 kHz apart.

121.5 MHz is the international emergency frequency, which every aircraft monitors.

ADS-B, 1090 MHz

Every airliner broadcasts its identity, position, altitude and speed every second. Flight-tracking websites are fed by thousands of volunteers' receivers: with a €30 SDR and a 7 cm antenna you can be one of them.

156–162 MHz · FM

⚓ Marine VHF

Ships, ports and rescue services talk on numbered FM channels. Channel 16 (156.800 MHz) is for distress and calling: everyone listens to it and moves to another channel to talk. Channel 70 is reserved for digital selective calling (DSC), the "red button" on ship radios.

AIS, 161.975 and 162.025 MHz

Ships broadcast their name, position, course and speed in very short digital bursts. That's how live ship-tracking maps work, also built from volunteers' receivers along the coast.

Signals falling from the sky

Satellites and balloons

Some of the easiest and most exciting signals to receive come from above. Amateur satellites and the Space Station are in Understand; here, the weather ones.

137 MHz

🛰️ Weather satellites

For decades the American NOAA satellites sent pictures of the clouds on 137 MHz that anyone could receive with a home-made antenna: they sounded like a rhythmic "tick-tock". They were switched off in 2025 (NOAA-18 in June, NOAA-19 on 13 August and NOAA-15 on 19 August).

The Russian Meteor-M satellites still transmit on the same band, digitally (LRPT): with an SDR, a satellite antenna and the right software you get colour images of your region as the satellite passes over.

400–406 MHz

🎈 Radiosondes

Twice a day, at 00 and 12 UTC, Spain's AEMET and weather services around the world release balloons carrying a radiosonde: a little box that measures temperature, humidity, pressure and wind as it climbs to 30 km, transmitting every second.

You can follow them live on collaborative maps such as SondeHub, fed by hobbyists. When the balloon bursts, the sonde falls by parachute and some people go and look for it: it's called "radiosonde hunting".

Learn to recognise them

How they sound

Tune through these bands with a receiver and you don't hear voices: you hear buzzes, screeches and bursts. Each system has its own "signature". Tap a signal to hear an imitation generated in your browser and see how it's drawn.

Before you switch on the receiver

What you may listen to (and what not)

Owning a receiver is legal. What matters is what you listen to and what you do with it. In Spain the Constitution protects the secrecy of communications, and the General Telecommunications Act treats intercepting communications not intended for the general public without authorisation as a very serious offence, and also disclosing what was heard. Most countries have similar rules.

✅ Free

  • Broadcasting: long, medium and short wave, FM, DAB+.
  • Radio amateurs: they talk in the open and like being listened to.
  • CB and PMR446: common use.
  • Beacons, amateur and weather satellites.
  • Radiosondes, aircraft ADS-B and ship AIS: position broadcasts designed for anyone to receive.

⚠️ Service communications

Aviation and marine voice, taxis, businesses, private security. Plenty of people tune in with scanners, but they are not intended for the public and Spanish law makes no exceptions: don't record them, don't share them and don't use what you hear.

⛔ No

  • Police, emergency and health services (TETRA, TETRAPOL, service DMR): they are also encrypted, and trying to decrypt them is far more serious.
  • Mobile phones, cordless phones and any private communication.

Seeing a signal on the waterfall isn't listening to its content: recognising a TETRA network by its shape is signal identification, not interception.

This is general guidance, not legal advice. And to transmit, outside the common-use bands, you need a licence: how to get one.

More range

Repeaters: how a signal gets further

On VHF and UHF, waves travel in a straight line and stop at the horizon or behind a hill. The solution is to put something very high up that receives and transmits again.

The repeater listens on one frequency and retransmits on another at the same time, with much more power and from a hilltop. Your handheld transmits on the input and listens on the output: your radio switches by itself if you set the offset.

The most important part of the station

Antennas

A radio without an antenna is deaf and dumb. The antenna decides how far you get and what you hear, far more than the radio does. And it's the most fun thing to build: with a well-cut piece of wire you can talk to other continents.

What is an antenna?

A piece of metal with just the right shape and size to turn an electric current flowing back and forth very fast into radio waves that travel through the air (when transmitting), and the other way round (when receiving). The same antenna does both.

It works like a guitar string: it vibrates best at the frequency it's sized for. That's why an antenna is roughly half a wavelength or a quarter long, and why shortwave antennas are huge while the one in your phone fits inside it.

Wavelength (m) = 300 ÷ frequency (MHz)

📏 How long is the antenna?

Theoretical lengths in free air; in practice you cut them about 5% shorter and trim. Detailed calculator in your first station.

What you need to know about any antenna

🎯 Resonance

An antenna "resonates" at one frequency, like the string. Away from it, the energy doesn't get out and comes back to the radio.

🔌 Impedance

The resistance it presents to the cable. Radios and cables use 50 Ω: if the antenna does too, all the power goes through.

📉 SWR

Standing wave ratio: how much energy bounces back. 1:1 is perfect, under 1.5 is very good, over 3 is bad.

🔦 Gain

An antenna doesn't create energy: it concentrates it in some directions, like a torch reflector. Measured in dBi (against an ideal antenna that radiates equally everywhere) or dBd (against a dipole). +3 dB is double.

🌸 Radiation pattern

The drawing of where it radiates. Omnidirectional: equally all round the horizon. Directional: strongly one way, weakly the others.

↕️ Polarisation

The orientation of the wave: vertical (handhelds, cars, repeaters), horizontal (long-distance VHF, TV) or circular (satellites). If it doesn't match, you lose a lot of signal.

📶 Bandwidth

How far you can move from the ideal frequency before the SWR rises too much. Thick antennas and broadband ones are more forgiving.

🏔️ Height and surroundings

On VHF and UHF every metre of height counts: the signal goes as far as the horizon. On shortwave, height above ground changes the angle at which it heads for the ionosphere.

👂 Receiving and transmitting

To listen, almost any wire will do. To transmit, the antenna must be tuned, or the radio protects itself by cutting power (or gets damaged).

⚡ Safety first

  • Never near power lines: neither while putting it up nor if it falls. That's the cause of serious antenna accidents.
  • Earthing and lightning protection for outdoor antennas. In a storm, disconnect the antenna from the radio.
  • Don't touch an antenna while it's transmitting: high-power ones can burn.
  • Roofs and neighbours: in Spain, licensed radio amateurs have the right to put up antennas on their building, with conditions. We explain it in your first station.
