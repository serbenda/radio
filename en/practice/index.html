Repeaters and digital voice

A handheld reaches a few kilometres. A repeater, installed on a hilltop, receives your signal on one frequency and retransmits it at the same time on another with much wider coverage: with a pocket handheld you can talk across your whole province. Almost every radio club runs one.

🔁 How to use one

  • Offset: you listen on the output frequency and transmit on another. On 2 m you transmit 600 kHz lower; on 70 cm, 7.6 MHz lower. Your radio does it for you once you set the offset (−).
  • Tone (CTCSS): many repeaters only open if your radio sends a specific sub-audible tone (for example 77.0 Hz). It is listed for each repeater.
  • To call in: listen for a while, then just give your callsign and say you are listening: “EA4XYZ listening”. If someone answers, off you go.
  • Etiquette: leave a pause of a couple of seconds between overs (in case someone has an emergency), don’t hog it, move to a direct (simplex) frequency for long chats, and identify every 10 minutes.

💻 Digital voice and the internet

Many repeaters are digital and linked over the internet: you talk on a handheld in Madrid and you’re heard in Buenos Aires. There are three systems, which don’t talk to each other:

  • DMR (the most widespread in Spain, affordable radios): you pick a talkgroup. On the BrandMeister network, 214 is Spain (Spain’s country code), 91 is worldwide and 9990 is a “parrot” that plays your audio back so you can test.
  • C4FM / Fusion (Yaesu) and D-STAR (Icom): similar, each with its own rooms.
  • EchoLink: links ordinary FM repeaters over the internet. With a licence you can use its phone app.
  • APRS on 144.800 MHz: your radio sends its position and short messages that appear on maps such as aprs.fi.

To find the repeaters near you, ask your radio club (the information changes often) or check URE’s lists and each network’s groups. All of this needs a licence to transmit; listening is free.
